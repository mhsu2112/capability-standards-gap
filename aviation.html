Aviation: the tombstone agency pattern against a 2,500× growing system
Innovations, consortiums, incidents, and regulations plotted against US airline revenue passenger miles (annual)
NTSB board member James B. King first used the term "tombstone agency" in 1977 to describe the FAA's pattern of enacting safety reform only after catastrophic accidents. The phrase was popularized by DOT Inspector General Mary Schiavo after the 1996 ValuJet 592 crash and tested empirically by Lutte and Bowen (2000), who found FAA rulemaking activity rises sharply in the months after major US accidents and decays in between. Each major regulatory action in this panel corresponds to a specific crash. The rare exceptions — the industry-led consortiums (ARINC, RTCA, IATA, CAST) and the international Chicago Convention — represent the anti-tombstone model: pre-incident coordination on technical standards and shared infrastructure.

Framework: the "tombstone agency" concept originated with NTSB board member James B. King (1977) and was tested empirically in Rebecca K. Lutte and Brent D. Bowen, "The FAA: A Tombstone Agency? Putting the Nickname to the Test," Collegiate Aviation Review 18:1 (2000). Diane Vaughan's The Challenger Launch Decision (University of Chicago Press, 1996) supplies the complementary "normalization of deviance" framework applicable to the 737 MAX MCAS certification failures. RPM data: pre-1960 from the BTS historical airmiles compilation (sourced from Statistical Abstracts); 1960–2000 from BTS National Transportation Statistics; 2000–present from FRED series RPM.
