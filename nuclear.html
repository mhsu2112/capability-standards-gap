Nuclear power: the negative case — arrested growth and tombstone consortiums
Innovations, consortiums, incidents, and regulations plotted against US installed nuclear capacity (gigawatts)
US civilian nuclear power was widely expected, in the 1950s and 1960s, to dominate electricity generation by the end of the century. It did not. Joseph V. Rees's Hostages of Each Other (Chicago, 1994) documents the surprising success of INPO as a "communitarian regulation" body — but INPO was created in December 1979, nine months after Three Mile Island, and was scoped narrowly to operational safety. It is the textbook tombstone consortium. The middle-governance layer that might have addressed the broader liftoff barriers — public-trust collapse, capital-cost compounding, siting and licensing fragmentation, waste storage — never cohered. No pre-incident industry-government coordination body ever existed at the scale of CAST (aviation) or DTCC (finance) or IETF (communications). Growth arrested in the early 1990s and the US fleet has slightly contracted since. This is the negative case: a sector where the engineering worked, the early policy worked (Atoms for Peace, Price-Anderson), but the middle-layer institutional plumbing was either absent or built too late.

Framework: Joseph V. Rees, Hostages of Each Other: The Transformation of Nuclear Safety Since Three Mile Island (University of Chicago Press, 1994). Rees treats INPO as a successful case of industry self-regulation under the "communitarian regulation" model — every plant's license is hostage to every other plant's safety performance. The negative-case argument here builds on Rees: INPO's success at operational safety did not translate into broader sector liftoff because (a) the body was created post-incident rather than proactively, (b) its scope was narrow (operations and training, not licensing, siting, capital coordination, or public-trust building), and (c) it operated alongside an NRC that was increasingly oriented to permitting friction. Capacity data: US Energy Information Administration historical electric power monthly; pre-1970 from EIA historical statistics. Bar widths normalized to peak 2011 capacity of ~101 GW. Bar lengths slightly understate the gap between expected and realized liftoff: 1970s projections from the AEC anticipated 1,000+ GW of US nuclear capacity by 2000.
