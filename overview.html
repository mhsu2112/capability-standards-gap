Overview: capability, adoption, and regulation — four industries, four shapes
A synthesis sketch across the four sector pages. Illustrative shapes, not literal data.
The thesis: capability, adoption, and regulation develop at different rates, and the shape of the gap between them is what shapes industry trajectory — sometimes through crisis, sometimes through arrested growth, sometimes through quiet periods. The four panels below are hand-drawn sketches of those shapes, meant to be read against the literal timelines in aviation, communications, finance, and nuclear.

Aviation — the tombstone pattern

Regulation moves only after fatal incidents. Gap reopens between crashes.

Shape: regulation moves as a staircase, each step tied to a tombstone. The gap between adoption and regulation widens silently, then snaps closed at each crash. The anti-tombstone exceptions — ARINC, RTCA, CAST — show what pre-incident coordination could have looked like.

Communications — industry rule-making takes over

Three Spar cycles; the source of "rules" shifts from state to industry.

Shape: capability and adoption ride an exponential. Regulation rises in the telegraph / radio cycles, then flattens — the state stops being the primary rule-maker. What fills the gap is the green line: industry rules (IETF, W3C, 3GPP), which track capability much more closely than statute does.

Finance — the Quiet Period closed the gap

Co-regulation tracked capability for 73 years; shadow banking reopened it.

Shape: three lines that converged in 1934 and held together for 73 years — the rarest pattern. The middle-governance layer (NYSE, NASD/FINRA, FASB, DTCC, ISDA) is what kept regulation close to capability. The gap reopened only when capability moved outside the regulated perimeter into shadow banking.

Nuclear — capability without adoption

Regulation became friction; adoption arrested at ~10% of projected.

Shape: the inverse pathology — capability outran adoption and never came back. Regulation, post-TMI, became a ceiling rather than a guardrail. INPO worked as a tombstone consortium for operational safety but was scoped too narrowly to address licensing, siting, or capital-cost compounding. The middle layer that worked in finance never cohered here.

Reading the shapes.   capability   adoption   regulation   industry / consortium (where it shows up). The gap between curves is the unit of analysis. In aviation, the regulation curve is a staircase tied to fatalities. In communications, a fourth line — industry rule-making — supplies the order the state stopped providing. In finance, the three lines actually tracked together for most of a century thanks to co-regulatory SROs; the gap only reopened when capability migrated outside the perimeter. In nuclear, regulation rose above adoption and adoption stalled — the pathology of a missing or too-narrow middle-governance layer.
